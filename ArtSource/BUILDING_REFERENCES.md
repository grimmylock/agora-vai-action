## What the first search found (2026-11-25; thin, so treat as starting points)
- **Bazaar / souk:** looks chaotic but follows trade clustering (like goods together; Ottoman bazaars had passages given to one craft); narrow alleys with small stalls on both sides, shaded by a patchwork of awnings; grows organically in modules, never redrawn. A post-apocalyptic market reads as a labyrinth of makeshift stalls, scrap heaps and bright tarps. Fallout 4's Nuka-Town market is walked as a loop from the entrance: livestock pen, weapon and general stalls, workbenches. Sources: wikipedia Bazaar/Suq, historyrise.com Ottoman marketplaces, connollycove.com, fables.gg post-apocalyptic world entries, gamerguides.com Fallout 4 Nuka-Town market.
- **Bar / pub:** about 3 ft (one tile) clear behind the counter for staff; about 2 ft of counter per seated customer; the counter and stools along one wall, booths or small tables along the opposite wall, round four-chair tables on the open floor; short logical paths between bar and seating; a back-of-house (kitchen/store) behind the bar. Sources: pos.toasttab.com bar floor plans, restaurant.eatapp.co, dimensions.com bar, critical-hits.com "architect DM".
- **Natural dressing:** "chaos with logic" - clusters with a reason (a stool by a stove, crates by a stall), uneven spacing, empty space kept on purpose; avoid grids and mirrored halves (Dishonored 2 level designer, 80.lv; gamedesignskills.com environmental storytelling).

## Rules we take from it
1. **Cluster by function, with a reason**: arms next to armour, medical alone, the black market tucked in a corner behind crates; the bar's kegs behind the counter, the stove with armchairs at the cold end.
2. **Goods and mess round every stall**: crates, sacks, barrels, a stool for the seller, a notice board on the wall; keep the queue space in front open.
3. **Counter = L or island with a staff aisle one tile wide**, shelves and kegs behind it, stools pulled out unevenly on the customer side.
4. **Furniture goes on walls or in groups, never single props floating in the middle**; tables in 2-3 with different spacing; space for a pool table to be played (about one tile clear round it).
5. **No mirrored or evenly stepped rows**; vary gaps (2-3-1), offset neighbouring stalls by a row.
6. Props cannot rotate yet (one south-facing sprite each): keep shelves and counters visually backed by something, and use the sprites' fronts toward the open floor.

## Place 1: Perimeter start area (2026-11-26): roadside station, farmstead, army field camp, checkpoint
Sources (text searches, thin): pos.toasttab.com / plan7architect.com / partstown.com gas-station and convenience-store design; britannica.com "farm building", core.ac.uk "Arranging the Buildings in the Farmstead", resilience.org "anatomy of a homestead landscape"; horizonstructures.com / equisearch.com barn layouts; chestofbooks.com camp layout, FM 3-19.40, a company camp SOP.
- **Roadside station:** pump islands out front under a canopy, parallel to the road with a way in and a way out; the shop door faces the pumps; sales floor with shelves in a loop and the cold drinks at the far side, the register by the door with a view of the whole floor; behind it a stock room and a toilet; a small garage bay or workbench at the end for the cheap repairs rural stations did.
- **Farmstead:** the farmyard is the centre, buildings face it (U or rectangle); the house sits apart, toward the road, with a yard or garden; the barn downwind of the house, silos at the end of the barn, a water source and a woodpile near the house; a tractor or cart parked in the open, haybales and a trough near the barn.
- **Barn:** a central aisle with stalls facing each other across it, a hayloft overhead (hay stored away from the stalls), a tack room across from the stalls, a workbench and tool wall, feed bins; a cowshed is a row of stalls along both long walls with troughs.
- **Field camp:** tents or huts in rows with even gaps and guy-line room, the command tent near the road with space to park, the field kitchen and mess apart from the sleeping huts, the latrine downhill and about 30 m from the kitchen but not far from the beds, a motor pool, sandbag walls and guard posts on the perimeter with a patrol lane inside.
- **Checkpoint:** a barrier across the road, a guard booth beside it (desk, stove, radio, bunk), a second shelter or sandbag nest on the far side, jersey barriers making the lane a zig-zag, a lamp and a sign.

## Lesson from place 1 (Yan, 2026-11-26: "it doesn't look like a gas station at all, the walls, the tiles, the furniture placement")
The first pass used layout principles only (loop aisle, register by the door, back room) and the game's default dark mossy plaster, grey slab floor and generic shelves: nothing said "gas station". The text searches never give pictures, so before building a kind of place, **write its visual identity down first and make every item real**: (1) the silhouette (gas station: a low flat-roofed box with a glass front and a big flat CANOPY over the pumps, a tall price sign by the road); (2) the signature props (pumps in a row on an island, canopy, price sign, drinks coolers on the back wall, ice chest outside, snack rack, counter by the door); (3) the materials (pale painted stucco with a coloured fascia band, shop-front glass, white floor tile, grey concrete forecourt); (4) what would be missing if you took the building away (the canopy and pumps still say "gas station"). New wall/floor/props are made for it if the game has none. Gas station sources: thc.texas.gov "Field Guide to Gas Stations in Texas" (box with canopy, pumps on separate islands, large glass, signage), archdaily "Evolution of Gas Stations", scottsdaleaz.gov gas station design guidelines, nrsplus / gilbarco c-store layouts (coolers along the back wall, gondolas perpendicular to them, register near the door).

## Place 2: Fishing Village (2026-11-26)
Sources (text): wikipedia "Fishing village" and "Stilt house/Palafito", concellofisterra.com Fisterra planning document, atlasobscura "Funaya of Ine" and "Hastings net huts", marcachile.cl palafitos of Chiloe, ferrovial stilt houses.
- Siting: around a small sheltered harbour with a safe way to land fish and tie boats; narrow lanes run parallel to the shore, crossed by paths down to the water.
- Houses: small, with room for gear, wood on piles at the water's edge (palafitos); the boat house keeps boats on the ground floor and nets on the loft above (funaya); net shops / net huts stand near the water.
- Work areas: drying racks and a smokehouse near the water, a cleaning table and crates at the jetty root, traps and floats stacked by the sheds.
- Visual identity (checklist): silhouette = low grey plank houses along a shoreline with jetties and boats; props = rowboat, fish drying rack, net on posts, crab pots, fish crates and tub, cleaning table, barrels; materials = sea-bleached grey driftwood boards with a tarred black foot, dark plank floors; the marsh keeps it damp and muted (the toxic water stays, nothing bright).

## Place 3: Scrapyard (2026-11-26)
Sources (text, thin): wikipedia "Wrecking yard", recyclingtoday "The lay of the land", ISRI scrap articles, model-railroad scrapyard threads, NATSO truck stop floor layout, toasttab diner floor plans.
- Yard: an office/shack by the entrance, a crusher, cars stacked in rows, scrap sorted by kind, a workshop for the yard's own equipment, a guard dog or two by the gate, a cutting/welding spot.
- Truck stop: the diner is for solo drivers at night (a counter with stools, booths on the windows, a kitchen run behind), separate car and truck lanes, direct walk from the lot to the door, a repair garage and diesel pumps.
- Visual identity: scrap = rust-orange and blue-grey junk, corrugated metal, tyres, oil drums, forge and anvil; the Scrap King's throne is junk (car seats, steering wheel, hubcaps); the diner reuses the stucco-and-red-band look with a lit DINER sign on a pole.

## Place: department store (2026-11-27)
Search found little on floor plans (retail design history, beauty counters by the entrance on the ground floor, Retail Dive / Glossy). What we took: a storefront of big display windows with a hanging sign and glass double doors; cosmetics-style display counters in rows near the front; paying counters (checkouts) by the entrance; customer service desk; mannequins by the windows; fitting rooms; at the back, away from customers: stock room with a loading door, office, staff room and tiled restrooms. One floor only (Yan: "we'll leave it at one floor"; escalators and lift removed).
Fitting rooms (searched 2026-11-28; sources: accessibility rule 521 CMR 33, fitsmallbusiness.com fitting-room design tips, coohom changing-room guides): a row of single-person cubicles side by side along a wall, each about 20-35 sq ft, closed by a curtain or louvre door facing the shop floor, a hook and a bench inside, and a mirror outside the cubicles (offset so it is not seen from the doorway) with a bench to wait on. The earlier walled block with side doors read as "doors to nowhere" because the front wall hid the cubicles' interior; now they are curtained booth props in a row.
